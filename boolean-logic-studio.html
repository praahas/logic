BOOLEAN LOGIC STUDIO
Two symbols. One idea. Every computer.

How true and false
learned to run a machine

Boolean algebra is the mathematics of yes and no — of switches that are open or closed. This is the story of how a 19th-century logician's system of reasoning became the physical language inside every processor, and a workshop for teaching it.

1854

Boole writes the Laws of Thought

George Boole proposes an algebra where variables are only 1 or 0 — true or false — combined with AND, OR, and NOT. Pure logic, no machine in sight.

1886

Peirce spots the switch

Charles Sanders Peirce notes that switches in series and parallel behave like AND and OR. Circuit sketches are drawn — but never published. The idea sleeps for 50 years.

1937–38

Shannon connects algebra to wire

Operating MIT's relay-driven Differential Analyzer, 21-year-old Claude Shannon sees that a relay's open/closed states are Boole's true/false. His master's thesis, A Symbolic Analysis of Relay and Switching Circuits, makes circuit design a deductive science.

1948

The bit is born

Shannon's information theory reframes the same binary choice as the fundamental unit of information — the bit. The relay that clicks and the message that is sent become one idea.

"It is possible to perform complex mathematical operations by means of relay circuits." — the closing idea of Shannon's 1938 thesis, the concept beneath every digital computer.
